# Diamond 2023 — Detection of large-scale cloud microphysical changes within a major shipping corridor after IMO 2020

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.5194/acp-23-8259-2023 (Atmos. Chem. Phys. 23, 8259–8278) — local copy: `raw/diamond-2023-imo2020-corridor.pdf`
**Date ingested:** 2026-08-09
**Type:** paper

## Summary

The "after" picture to [[diamond-2020-shipping-corridor]]. Using the same universal-kriging counterfactual over the southeast Atlantic corridor (18–8°S, 13°W–8°E), with 5,000 simulated no-ship scenarios conditioned on meteorology, Diamond tested whether the [[imo-2020-regulation]] left a detectable fingerprint in cloud microphysics.[^1] It did: the cloud-top effective-radius perturbation in 2020–2022 was unprecedentedly weak — "an unambiguous decrease in the magnitude of the re perturbation within the shipping corridor is evident in the post-regulation (2020–2022) data."[^2] Albedo changes point the same way but with less statistical confidence (90% level in austral spring).[^3]

Two framings matter. First, monitoring clouds works as compliance verification — the corridor going microphysically quiet is independent geophysical evidence that ships actually switched fuels.[^4] Second, the radiative bookkeeping: shipping had been forcing corridor clouds by ~2 W/m² in austral spring (~0.5 W/m² annually), and the post-2020 data imply a 35–70% decline in that warming offset, consistent with a global forcing change of order 0.1 W/m².[^5]

## Key Takeaways

- Post-IMO-2020 droplet-size perturbation in the corridor is outside all prior confidence intervals — a detected microphysical change.[^2]
- Cloud brightening decreased; albedo signal detected at 90% confidence in austral spring.[^3]
- Corridor IRFaci ~2 W/m² (austral spring), ~0.5 W/m² annual; implied global change O(0.1 W/m²), a 35–70% decline in the shipping warming offset.[^5]
- Cloud observations can verify fuel-regulation compliance from space.[^4]
