# Feingold et al. 2024 — Physical science research needed to evaluate the viability and risks of marine cloud brightening

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adi8594 (Science Advances 10, eadi8594) — local copy: `raw/feingold-2024-mcb-roadmap.pdf`
**Date ingested:** 2026-08-09
**Type:** paper (consensus roadmap)

## Summary

The consensus research roadmap for deliberate [[marine-cloud-brightening]], authored by 31 atmospheric scientists led by Graham Feingold (NOAA CSL) with Lynn Russell (Scripps) and colleagues.[^1] Its premise: policymakers are starting to ask about MCB, but "policymakers currently don't have the information they need to reach decisions."[^2] The roadmap lays out what would be required to evaluate viability: laboratory aerosol–cloud microphysics work, long-running ocean-based field experiments, and modeling that spans from droplet scale to global scale — demonstrating that spray particles of the right size can reach clouds, form droplets, brighten consistently over large areas, and not backfire via cloud thinning.[^3]

[[ship-tracks]] are the roadmap's natural analog and cautionary tale in one: they prove aerosols can brighten marine clouds, but their high variability of occurrence shows the response depends delicately on meteorology, background aerosol, and cloud state — "the optimal particle size and amount is likely dependent on cloud properties that can change as they drift."[^4]

The wiki-level significance: the inadvertent experiment (IMO 2020) and the proposed deliberate one (MCB) are the same physics with opposite signs, and every detection problem documented in [[zhang-2025-hard-to-detect]] and [[gettelman-2024-brought-forward]] doubles as an MCB verification problem.

## Key Takeaways

- 31-scientist consensus roadmap: labs + field campaigns + multiscale modeling before MCB can be evaluated.[^1][^3]
- Ship tracks are the proof-of-concept and the warning about variability.[^4]
- MCB framed as possible harm reduction, not a substitute for decarbonization.[^5]
