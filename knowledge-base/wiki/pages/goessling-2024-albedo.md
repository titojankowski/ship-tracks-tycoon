# Goessling et al. 2024 — Recent global temperature surge amplified by record-low planetary albedo

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adq7280 (Science 387, 68–73); preprint local copy: `raw/goessling-2024-albedo.pdf`
**Date ingested:** 2026-08-09
**Type:** paper

## Summary

The energy-budget accounting of the 2023 anomaly, from AWI (Goessling, Rackow, Jung) using CERES satellite radiation data. In 2023 "the global mean temperature soared to 1.48K above the pre-industrial level, surpassing the previous record by 0.17K" — and known drivers (greenhouse warming plus emerging El Niño) fall roughly 0.2 K short of explaining it.[^1] Their answer for the gap: 2023 had the lowest planetary albedo in the satellite record, and "record-low planetary albedo" is "the primary factor bridging this gap."[^2]

The albedo decline "is caused largely by a reduced low-cloud cover in the northern mid-latitudes and tropics," continuing a multi-year trend.[^3] Crucially for this wiki, the paper stops short of attributing that low-cloud decline: candidate causes are internal variability, reduced aerosol concentrations (including the [[imo-2020-regulation]] cut), and a possibly emerging positive low-cloud feedback — with very different implications for the future depending on which dominates.[^4]

This puts Goessling between the poles of [[2023-warmth-attribution]]: it confirms a real, large shortwave/cloud contribution to 2023 (consistent with the direction Hansen argues) while declining to hand the cause to shipping specifically.

## Key Takeaways

- 2023: +1.48 K vs pre-industrial, beating the record by 0.17 K; ~0.2 K unexplained by standard drivers.[^1]
- Record-low planetary albedo closes the gap; driven by northern mid-latitude and tropical low-cloud decline.[^2][^3]
- Cause of the cloud decline left open: variability vs aerosol reduction vs cloud feedback.[^4]
