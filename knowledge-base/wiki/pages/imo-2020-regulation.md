# IMO 2020 Sulfur Regulation

## Description

The International Maritime Organization's global cap on marine fuel sulfur content, in force since 1 January 2020: maximum sulfur dropped from 3.5% to 0.5% by mass — roughly an 80–86% cut in allowed sulfur.[^1] The rule was an air-quality and public-health measure (marine heavy fuel oil was among the dirtiest fuels burned anywhere), and compliance appears to have been substantial — verifiable even from space, via the weakening cloud-microphysical perturbation in shipping corridors.[^2]

Its unintended consequence is this wiki's subject. Ship sulfate had been brightening marine clouds ([[ship-tracks]], [[twomey-effect]]) and thereby offsetting some greenhouse warming ([[aerosol-masking]]). Removing it abruptly constituted what the literature now calls an [[inadvertent-geoengineering]] termination — visible [[ship-tracks]] fell to their lowest recorded frequency in 2020,[^3] and published estimates of the resulting positive radiative forcing cluster around +0.07 to +0.2 W/m² (catalogued in [[imo2020-forcing-estimates]]), with model temperature responses near +0.05°C over the 2020s, concentrated in the North Atlantic, North Pacific, and Arctic.[^4]

Whether IMO 2020 contributed marginally or substantially to the record warmth of 2023–24 is the field's live controversy — see [[2023-warmth-attribution]].

Precursor rules matter for interpretation: regional Emission Control Areas (North Sea, Baltic, North American coasts, 0.1% since 2015) had already cleaned near-coastal shipping, so the 2020 global cap mostly perturbed open-ocean corridors.[^5]
