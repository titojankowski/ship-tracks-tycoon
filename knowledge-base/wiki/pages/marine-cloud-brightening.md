# Marine Cloud Brightening (MCB)

## Description

The proposed deliberate version of what ships did by accident: spray sub-micron sea-salt aerosol into shallow marine clouds to raise droplet numbers ([[twomey-effect]]), brighten the clouds, and reflect more sunlight — a regional-to-global cooling intervention. [[ship-tracks]] are its existence proof and its cautionary tale: they show brightening works, and that the response varies wildly with meteorology, background aerosol, and cloud state.[^1]

The state of play is defined by the 2024 consensus roadmap ([[feingold-2024-mcb-roadmap]], 31 authors): before MCB can be evaluated for deployment, the field needs laboratory microphysics, long-running ocean field experiments, and models that connect droplet scale to global scale — demonstrating particles of the right size can be delivered, brighten consistently, and not backfire through cloud thinning ([[cloud-adjustments]]).[^2] The University of Washington MCB Program (with NOAA and PNNL) runs the small-scale spray experiments; policymaker interest is ahead of the science.[^3]

The IMO 2020 experience feeds MCB two sobering lessons. Quantitatively: the shipping perturbation — comparable in kind to an MCB deployment — produced a forcing that took years to detect at all, with regional detection rates of 12–40%; a deliberate program "will need to be substantial in order to overcome the low detectability."[^4] Institutionally: if attributing a known perturbation is this hard, verifying and governing a deliberate one is harder ([[gettelman-2024-brought-forward]]).[^5] The pre-2020 shipping era also bounds the achievable: an accidental global fleet-scale program yielded ~−1 W/m² in low clouds at most ([[diamond-2020-shipping-corridor]]).[^6]
