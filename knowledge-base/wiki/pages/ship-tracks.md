# Ship Tracks

## Description

Ship tracks are linear features of brightened cloud that form in low marine clouds along shipping routes: aerosol particles in ship exhaust act as extra cloud condensation nuclei, so the same cloud water condenses onto more, smaller droplets, raising the cloud's albedo (the [[twomey-effect]]). They were first reported from early weather-satellite imagery by Conover in 1966 as "anomalous cloud lines," and Twomey supplied the brightening mechanism a few years later.[^1] They occur preferentially in the great subtropical stratocumulus decks (southeast Atlantic, southeast and northeast Pacific), where shallow clouds over dark ocean give aerosol perturbations their largest radiative leverage.[^2]

Ship tracks matter for two reasons. Scientifically, each one is a controlled aerosol-injection experiment run by accident, making them the sharpest available probe of aerosol–cloud interactions ([[twomey-effect]], [[cloud-adjustments]]) — the largest single uncertainty in anthropogenic radiative forcing.[^3] The visible track is only the tip: plume-following analysis shows ship aerosol changes cloud properties substantially "even when no ship tracks are visible," and visible-only studies carried a meteorological selection bias.[^4] A deep-learning census found tracks were ~10× more common than manual surveys suggested.[^5]

Practically, ship tracks were a globally distributed cooling infrastructure — until the [[imo-2020-regulation]] cut fuel sulfur by ~86% in 2020 and visible tracks collapsed to their lowest recorded frequency.[^5] Their disappearance is the natural experiment driving the entire post-2020 literature ([[inadvertent-geoengineering]]), and their physics is the template for deliberate [[marine-cloud-brightening]].[^6]
