# Yuan et al. 2022 — Global reduction in ship-tracks from sulfur regulations for shipping fuel

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abn7988 (Science Advances 8, eabn7988) — local copy (preprint): `raw/yuan-2022-ship-track-climatology.pdf`
**Date ingested:** 2026-08-09
**Type:** paper

## Summary

The first global, multi-year climatology of [[ship-tracks]], built by running deep-learning detection models over decades of satellite imagery rather than hand-cataloguing tracks.[^1] The automated census found ship tracks far more common than earlier manual surveys had suggested — more than ten times higher frequency than expected from a previous survey.[^2]

Its central observational result: after the [[imo-2020-regulation]] took effect (compounded by COVID-19 shipping slowdowns), ship-track frequency in 2020 dropped to its lowest level in the record, across every ocean basin.[^3] Track density also responds to earlier, regional sulfur rules (Emission Control Areas), and route patterns shift with fuel-cost economics — ship tracks double as an economic tracer of shipping activity.[^4]

From the track reduction the authors bracket the aerosol indirect forcing induced by the fuel regulation at 0.02–0.22 W/m² (warming), the first observation-based range for what removing ship sulfur does to Earth's energy budget.[^5] This is the empirical bridge between the regulation and the forcing estimates later refined by [[yuan-2024-termination-shock]], [[gettelman-2024-brought-forward]], and [[jordan-henry-2024-ukesm1]].

## Key Takeaways

- Deep learning over satellite data → first multi-year global ship-track climatology.[^1]
- Ship tracks were ~10× more frequent than earlier manual surveys estimated.[^2]
- 2020 ship-track frequency fell to the lowest level in recent decades globally after IMO 2020 (+ pandemic effects).[^3]
- Regulation-induced aerosol indirect forcing bracketed at +0.02 to +0.22 W/m².[^5]
